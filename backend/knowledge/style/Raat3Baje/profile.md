## His voice in one paragraph
He talks like a first-person storyteller sitting close to the mic, telling it as something that happened to him. He starts with a polite greeting and a promise ("Toh chaliye ab bina waqt zaya kiye aaj ki kahani ko sunte hain."). He gives the time and place as plain facts ("Yeh baat 1999 ki hai. December ka mahina tha."). He narrates the fear in small, literal, physical steps ("Mere gardan se khoon beh raha tha. Aankhein laal thi."). The Hindi is Roman and fairly formal-spoken, with words like "gasht," "aadesh," "nirnay" and "bandobast." English appears only as everyday nouns ("jeep," "duty," "torch," "post-mortem"). He turns to the viewer as "doston" at a few chosen moments, such as the personal aside "Par sach kahoon doston," and the closing moral "Doston, zaroori nahi ki...". On the recordings he shortens and roughens bookish lines into plain speech.

## How he tells a story and builds tension
- Start with facts in order: when, where, what job: "Meri pehli posting mere ghar se bahut door ki gayi thi."
- Build dread by small logical steps, not adjectives: tiredness, then refusal to leave, then the warnings.
- Others warn him. The warning is quoted, and he doubts it: "Sar, yeh jungle raat mein rukne ke liye accha nahi hai." Then: "Mujhe un dono ki baatein sunkar un par aur shak ho raha tha."
- Use a rationalising line, then the fear returns: "Yahan har waqt aisi aawazein aati rehti hain."
- The scare is literal body detail: "Laga jaise koi meri chhati par baith meri gardan ko apne nakhunon se kured raha ho."
- Say the character cannot move or does not understand: "Main uth nahi pa raha tha."
- A cut line sets up the next chapter: "Uski woh baat"
- A recorded variant uses the same repeated sentence frame: "Doosre hafte, usne pehli baar kadmon ki awaaz suni." / "Teesre hafte..."
- Backstory comes at the end as a reveal with its own title: "Sachayi ka khulasa."

- He replaces abstract place words with concrete ones: "ke aakhri mod" became "mein chaar mor" (corrected: "chaar mod par"). He swaps the vague "aakhri" for a specific number.
- Pattern summary:
  - Drops formal or poetic vocabulary.
  - Keeps the sentence shorter.
  - Prefers concrete words (a number, a thing) to abstract ones.
  - Uses "mein" and "aur" for smoother speech.
